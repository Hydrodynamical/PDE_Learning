\title{\textbf{Can Language Models Learn a PDE?}\\[5pt]
\large An Interactive Benchmark for Experimental Design\\
in Weak-Form Coefficient Identification}
\author{Joseph K. Miller\\
\small Department of Mathematics, Massachusetts Institute of Technology}
\date{\small Experiments: April 2026 \qquad Revised: October 2026}

\begin{abstract}
We introduce an interactive benchmark in which language models choose measurements
to identify the coefficients of a one-dimensional elliptic equation. The environment
provides access to a numerical solution, budgeted weak-form queries, and free
diagnostic tools. Numerical linear algebra is delegated to a solver; the model
selects test functions, interprets feedback, and decides when to submit.
We reanalyze a public archive of 180 scored runs from six models, three difficulty
levels, and ten shared problem seeds per configuration. Recovery rates vary
substantially across models and instances, while action counts, within-session
improvement, query-family diversity, and confidence reports reveal differences
beyond the final error. We regenerate the figures from the archived logs and
correct a stale success count in a previously checked-in figure.
The diagnostics describe observable behavior rather than validated cognitive
faculties. We also distinguish numerical conditioning from structural
identifiability: a single solution need not uniquely determine the operator, even
within the benchmark's finite-dimensional coefficient class. The archive and
reproduction script support verification of the reported aggregates; incomplete
attempt histories and provider settings limit broader comparisons.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An experiment can be difficult because the available measurements are poorly
chosen, even when the computations following those measurements are routine.
This distinction motivates a benchmark in which a language model directs an
inverse-problem investigation. It can request measurements, inspect coefficient
estimates, test consistency, and revise its choices. The computational tools
perform integration and least-squares recovery, leaving measurement selection and
the use of feedback as the model's main responsibilities.

The task builds on the weak formulation of an elliptic boundary value
problem \citep{evans2010pde}. It also connects to active data selection
\citep{mackay1992information}: the informative value of a measurement depends on
the measurements already collected. A small residual does not necessarily imply
accurate coefficients, and visibly different test functions need not add
independent constraints. These features provide concrete opportunities to study
how models respond to numerical evidence.

The benchmark was introduced in a Kaggle write-up \citep{kaggle2026pde}, with
behavioral interpretations motivated by the cognitive framework of
\citet{burnell2026cognitive}. Here we organize the task around three contributions:
an instrumented interface for tool-assisted coefficient identification;
a reproducible descriptive analysis of 180 archived scored runs; and an account
of the numerical and interpretive limits of that analysis. The question in the
title concerns identification during a session, not training model weights.
The behavioral measurements are hypotheses about useful aspects of scientific
problem solving, not independent validation of general cognitive abilities.

\section{Task and numerical interface}
\label{sec:task}

\subsection{Hidden equation and measurement system}

The hidden boundary value problem is
\begin{equation}
  -(a(x)u'(x))'+b(x)u'(x)+c(x)u(x)=f(x),\qquad x\in[0,1],
  \quad u(0)=u(1)=0.
  \label{eq:pde}
\end{equation}
Each coefficient \(q\in\{a,b,c,f\}\) is represented by
\(q(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\theta_{q,j}P_j(x)\), where \(P_j\) denotes the
ordinary shifted Legendre polynomial on \([0,1]\). These polynomials are
orthogonal, but are not normalized to have unit \(L^2\) norm.
The environment samples the coefficients and computes a numerical solution.
The model accesses \(u\) and \(u'\) through an evaluation tool.

For a test function \(\varphi_k\) vanishing at both endpoints, integration by
parts gives
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1 au'\varphi_k'\dd x+
 \int_0^1 bu'\varphi_k\dd x+
 \int_0^1 cu\varphi_k\dd x
 =r_k,\qquad
 r_k=\int_0^1 f\varphi_k\dd x.
 \label{eq:weak}
\end{equation}
Define the basis-weighted matrices
\begin{align}
 D_{kj}&=\int_0^1P_j u'\varphi_k'\dd x,&
 B_{kj}&=\int_0^1P_j u'\varphi_k\dd x,\nonumber\\
 C_{kj}&=\int_0^1P_j u\varphi_k\dd x,&
 F_{kj}&=\int_0^1P_j\varphi_k\dd x.
 \label{eq:matrices}
\end{align}
After \(K\) queries, the solver separately minimizes
\(\|A\theta-r\|_2\), with \(A=[D\mid B\mid C]\in\R^{K\times3N}\),
and \(\|F\theta_f-r\|_2\).
The model chooses measurements; it does not supply its own least-squares
implementation. The interface requires at least \(4N\) stored queries before
solving. This is a protocol rule, not a guarantee of rank or uniqueness.

\section{Recovery, conditioning, and identifiability}
\label{sec:identifiability}

For each coefficient block, the reported error is the absolute maximum
coefficient deviation,
\begin{equation}
 E_q=\max_{0\leq j<N}|\widehat\theta_{q,j}-\theta_{q,j}|,
 \qquad E=E_a+E_b+E_c+E_f.
 \label{eq:error}
\end{equation}
The main success threshold is \(E<0.01\), with \(E<0.1\) reported as a
sensitivity check. These thresholds match the archived scoring and plotting code.
They are not thresholds on function error at held-out spatial points, as stated
in the original write-up. The scorer separately evaluates function errors on a
500-point grid. Since \(|P_j(x)|\leq1\), the total function error is bounded above
by \(NE\), but coefficient and function thresholds of the same numerical value
are not equivalent.

Recoverability depends on the joint design matrix \(A\), not only on the number
or appearance of the test functions. Full column rank gives unique
least-squares operator coefficients; small singular values make estimates
sensitive to quadrature, discretization, and roundoff. Separate block condition
numbers can reveal weak contributions but cannot rule out dependencies
\emph{between} blocks. The source is recovered from a different matrix \(F\);
its small observed errors should not be attributed to linearity alone, since all
four recovery problems are linear in their unknown coefficients.

The reaction weights can be small when \(u\) is small, but their relative
magnitude is instance-dependent. A large unweighted reaction integral does not
ensure that a proposed row adds independent information. Screening term weights
is therefore a possible heuristic, not an optimal-design guarantee. Similarly,
a low consistency residual can coexist with substantial coefficient error.

\begin{remark}[A single solution need not identify the operator]
Take \(u(x)=x(1-x)\) and \(f(x)=2\).
For any sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\), the coefficients
\[
 a=1+\varepsilon,\qquad
 b=\varepsilon(4x-2),\qquad
 c=-8\varepsilon
\]
satisfy the same boundary value problem because
\[
 2\varepsilon+
 \varepsilon(4x-2)(1-2x)-8\varepsilon x(1-x)=0.
\]
These perturbations lie in the \(N=2\) coefficient class. For example,
\(\varepsilon=-1/8\) preserves positive diffusion and gives nonnegative
reaction. No choice of test functions can distinguish these operators from
\((a,b,c)=(1,0,0)\) using this solution and source alone.
\end{remark}

Thus the benchmark does not have unconditional structural identifiability.
Uniqueness results using boundary maps or time-dependent observations
\citep{egger2014simultaneous,browning2024structural,browning2025parabolic}
concern different observation operators and do not establish it here.
A stronger benchmark should characterize joint rank on its sampled instances,
include nonidentifiable cases explicitly, and test additional sources or
boundary data where uniqueness is required.

\section{Archived study and provenance}
\label{sec:data}

The public Kaggle dataset, \emph{Elliptic Differential Equation LLM Reasoning},
version 1, contains 180 scored run logs and an aggregate summary, released on
16 April 2026. There are six model IDs, three difficulties, and exactly seeds
1--10 in every cell (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Ground-truth coefficients agree
across models for each seed--difficulty pair, permitting comparisons on the
same ten tasks. The selected archive contains scored records; it does not

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Coefficient recovery}

Figure~\ref{fig:success} summarizes the scored archive. At \(E<0.01\), Gemini 3.1
Pro succeeds on \(10/10\), \(9/10\), and \(10/10\) easy, medium, and hard instances.
Claude Opus 4.6 achieves \(10/10\), \(9/10\), and \(8/10\). GPT-5.4 has a sharper
decline, from \(7/10\) on easy to \(3/10\) on medium and \(1/10\) on hard.
The remaining models also show substantial task dependence. These are
descriptive rates on ten shared instances, conditional on inclusion in the
scored archive, rather than estimates of success on all attempted runs.

The regenerated strict-threshold figure corrects GPT-5.4 mini on hard from
\(0/10\) in the checked-in PNG to \(2/10\). Both the individual logs and archived
summary identify seeds 4 and 5 as below \(0.01\). The corrected count also shows
why model-wide rankings and claims about universal difficulty gradients should
be avoided. All other displayed success counts agree with the original assets.

\subsection{Tool use, improvement, and query diversity}

The write-up's behavioral figures address useful questions beyond final error,
provided their labels are interpreted operationally.

\paragraph{Action use.}
Figure~\ref{fig:actions} separates solve calls, consistency checks, and term-integral
requests. The profiles show how models allocate tool interactions. More activity
does not by itself imply better recovery: the common prompt encourages repeated
solves, checks return information without adding solver rows, and efficient
strategies can use few actions. The bars stack separate marginal medians, so the
height is not necessarily the median total action count.

\paragraph{Improvement during a session.}
For runs with successive solves, define
\(R=E_{\mathrm{first}}/E_{\mathrm{last}}\).
Figure~\ref{fig:improvement} shows substantial variation across seeds.
The original plotting convention assigns \(R=1\) when the improvement ratio is
missing, including single-solve runs. A large ratio may reflect a poor initial
estimate; it is not evidence of a small final error. Improvement with added
measurements also does not alone distinguish model adaptation from routine
least-squares improvement.

\centering

Dots show individual archived runs and black markers show medians across the ten

\paragraph{Query-family diversity.}
The implementation assigns expressions to five heuristic string-based families:
polynomial, trigonometric, exponential, localized, and other. We report
\[
 H_{\mathrm{norm}}=
 -\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{5}p_i\log_2p_i}{\log_2 5},
\]
where \(p_i\) is the fraction assigned to family \(i\) in the archived taxonomy
at the final solve, and \(0\log0=0\). Six runs queried again after their final
solve; those later queries do not enter this archived entropy.
Figure~\ref{fig:diversity} describes differences in this taxonomy.
The statistic does not measure linear independence: a diverse family mixture
can be redundant, while polynomial-only functions can span useful directions.
It should be interpreted alongside the joint matrix and final recovery error.

\centering

\caption{Normalized query-family entropy regenerated from the archive.
Bars show means, whiskers one population standard deviation, dots individual
runs, and black markers medians. The taxonomy is a heuristic classification of
expressions; entropy does not establish informative or independent measurements.}
\label{fig:diversity}

\subsection{Confidence reporting}

The interface requests confidence that each coefficient vector is within an
absolute error of \(0.1\). Appendix~\ref{app:confidence} examines empirical
agreement between these reports and the corresponding recorded estimates.
This differs from the write-up's fixed confidence-order diagnostic
\(\mathrm{Conf}(a)>\mathrm{Conf}(b)>\mathrm{Conf}(c)\), which assumes a hierarchy
that need not hold on every instance. It also differs from the legacy
design-matrix sensitivity diagnostics discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:legacy}.

The calibration analysis uses transcript order to pair reports only with
earlier solves, preventing a later improved estimate from being assigned to an
earlier confidence statement. Reports with no preceding solve are excluded,
and bin counts are retained. Repeated reports from the same run are dependent;
the curves are descriptive and are not used to rank general metacognitive ability.

The archive contains 733 reports, of which 707 have a preceding solve and
26 do not. The paired reports contribute 2,121 coefficient observations after
excluding the source block. The summary retains counts in disjoint width-0.1
confidence bins. Reports in one response are paired only to strictly earlier
solve outputs, rather than a solve requested in that same response. This corrects
the older plotting code's forward-looking query-count matching and overlapping
bin endpoints.

\section{Interpretation, limitations, and next experiments}

The benchmark isolates an interesting interaction between measurement selection
and numerical tools. It is related to applied-mathematics evaluations such as
HARDMath2 \citep{hardmath2025} and mathematical-modeling tasks such as Mamo
\citep{mamo2025}, while emphasizing sequential inverse-problem measurements.
Its behavioral axes are inspired by a cognitive taxonomy
\citep{burnell2026cognitive}; they do not establish that the task independently
measures eight cognitive faculties.

Several limits matter for interpretation. First, only ten shared PDE seeds per
difficulty are represented, and the archive includes scored records rather than
a complete attempt history. Second, a text-based interface mixes mathematical
decisions with command formatting and protocol compliance. A turn without a
recognized command is not equivalent to a rejected mathematical expression:
a recognized \texttt{QUERY} can still fail validation. Third, repeated solves,
confidence reporting, and iteration are explicitly prompted, so action counts
cannot establish unprompted planning.

Fourth, single-solution structural nonuniqueness can coexist with numerical
conditioning problems. Future evaluations should report joint rank and singular
values, distinguish identifiable from ambiguous cases, and compare recovery in
coefficient space with out-of-design predictions. Fifth, the archive does not
pin complete provider settings or implementation revisions. Reproducing the
saved figures is a different claim from reproducing historical model responses.

Useful next comparisons include fixed polynomial and trigonometric query
designs, random admissible queries, and adaptive design heuristics under identical
query and turn budgets. Baseline and standard prompts should be compared on
matched tasks with all attempts retained. Confidence evaluation should define
the correctness event in advance, retain report-level sample counts, and account
for dependence within runs \citep{guo2017calibration}. These controls would help
determine which behavioral differences predict useful experimental decisions.

\section{Conclusion}

Weak-form coefficient identification supplies a controlled setting for studying
how language models choose measurements and use numerical feedback. The recovered
180-run archive permits reproducible comparisons of final errors and concrete
interaction patterns. The results support descriptive differences among the
evaluated models on these tasks, while the identifiability counterexample and
provenance limits constrain stronger claims. The next step is a controlled
comparison of measurement strategies with explicit rank checks and complete
attempt histories.

\section{Legacy sensitivity and monitoring statistics}
\label{app:legacy}

The archived implementation computes a truncated pseudoinverse \(H_k\) of
\(A_k^\top A_k\), discarding singular values below \(10^{-10}\) times the
largest value. It forms block scores
\[
 s_k(q)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in q}(H_k)_{ii}},\qquad q\in\{a,b,c\}.
\]
These are design-based sensitivity proxies, not the model's true epistemic
uncertainty. Interpreting an inverse information matrix as a covariance requires
an appropriate observation-noise model and rank assumptions. The truncated
pseudoinverse can assign zero weight to unresolved directions.

The legacy monitoring routine compares rank encodings of \(s_k\) and stated
uncertainty. Its double-\texttt{argsort} ranking does not average tied ranks,
so it is not standard tie-aware Spearman correlation. The control routine applies
a Sherman--Morrison-style update to the truncated inverse and compares reductions
across coefficient blocks for the \emph{chosen} next row. This need not equal a
freshly recomputed truncated inverse, and it does not compare alternative query
choices. We retain these fields in the source logs but do not use them as
calibrated uncertainty measures or as evidence of optimal experimental design.
